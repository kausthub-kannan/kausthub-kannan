Society runs on a moral compass, driven either by one’s own sense of morality or enforced by society itself. This ensures the smooth running of the social cycle. But there are situations in which a sense of righteousness enables acts of disorder that threaten social stability itself.

To understand this, the best example to take is from the great epic, the Mahabharata. One of the most controversial questions that arises when reading it concerns the decisions made by Krishna. If one accepts Krishna’s nature as Ishvara, that resolves the question, to a certain extent, from a theological perspective. What I found particularly interesting, however, is that even without approaching the subject through that lens, Krishna’s decisions can be understood through a coherent ethical framework. This framework explains why certain situations demand that we prioritise and perform an act which sounds unrighteous, or adharmic, as the most righteous one.

In the Sauptika Parva, Ashwatthama raises several objections concerning violations of the conventions of warfare by the Pandavas. He uses these incidents to question whether the Pandavas themselves have adhered to the very dharma they claim to protect.

These are as follows:

1. Drona was killed by Dhrishtadyumna after the former had laid down his weapons. Prior to this, Yudhishthira had misled Drona by telling him that Ashwatthama was dead.
2. Arjuna killed Karna when the latter was incapacitated, attempting to free his chariot wheel.
3. Shikhandin was placed before Bhishma, knowing full well that Bhishma would not fight Shikhandin.
4. Satyaki killed Bhurishravas when the latter had withdrawn from the fight.
5. Duryodhana was killed by Bhima through a blow below the waist, violating the conventions of gada-yuddha.

Kripa was present when Ashwatthama raised these objections. The conversation takes place when Ashwatthama, consumed by rage following Duryodhana’s defeat, is contemplating the destruction of the Pandava camp. He eventually carries out the night massacre, killing the Upapandavas and other sleeping warriors.

There is little dispute that the above incidents involved violations of the conventions of warfare. The interesting part, however, is how Krishna responds to the accusations concerning Karna and Duryodhana. I would like to focus on these two, as the underlying reasoning helps us understand the others as well.

When Karna and Duryodhana appeal to the conventions of warfare, Krishna responds by recalling two important incidents: Draupadi’s vastraharana and Abhimanyu’s cruel death.

At first glance, Krishna’s justification might appear to be: “You violated dharma, so the Pandavas cannot be blamed for violating it either.” But that would be a rather superficial reading of his argument. His reasoning makes much more sense when examined through a familiar example.

Following the same thread of thought, consider Draupadi’s vastraharana.

The ill-minded Kurus laughed and proceeded with the despicable act of humiliating Draupadi. This episode is rightly regarded as one of the greatest acts of cruelty in the epic. What makes it particularly disturbing is the silence of Bhishma, Drona and the other noble elders present in the assembly.

Bhishma himself responds by pointing to the difficulty of discerning dharma and how sukshma (subtle) its nature can be. These noble warriors found themselves caught between competing obligations: loyalty to the throne, allegiance to the kingdom, their commitments and their understanding of their respective duties. They chose to give precedence to these obligations. Yes, these obligations were themselves part of dharma. But at what cost? Was the dharma they chose to uphold appropriate when weighed against the adharma being committed before them? Were these obligations of equal moral significance? Clearly, they were not.

This brings us back to Karna and Duryodhana. Had Arjuna and Bhima allowed themselves to be restrained by the conventional obligations of warfare in these particular circumstances, they would have risked allowing a greater adharma to prevail by failing to defeat individuals who had repeatedly and knowingly participated in it.

Karna and Duryodhana had been given multiple opportunities to reconsider their actions. Yet they continued to participate in and perpetuate adharma. The conventions of warfare, which were intended to regulate combat between warriors, could now become a shield behind which such individuals continued to benefit.

This is precisely where Krishna’s recollection of the earlier incidents becomes significant. In the assembly, the invocation of dharma and competing obligations effectively allowed adharma to flourish. If the conventions of dharma were invoked once again without considering the larger moral circumstances, they could end up protecting the very individuals responsible for perpetuating adharma.

Krishna’s argument, therefore, makes much more sense understood this way, rather than reduced to a simple law of cancellation: “You violated dharma, so we can violate it too.” The argument is not that the wrongdoing of one party automatically permits wrongdoing by another. Rather, it is that adherence to a particular obligation cannot be considered righteous when doing so would enable a greater violation of dharma.

One important question remains: how was Krishna able to decide which action was more morally right?

I believe Krishna provides a compelling justification by recalling these past incidents. He demonstrates how, on previous occasions, the invocation of dharma, in the form of loyalty and institutional obligations, allowed adharma to flourish. He brings these incidents back to Arjuna’s attention so that he can understand why blindly adhering to the conventions of warfare in the present circumstances could lead to a similar outcome.

This leads us back to the start of this blog. The subtle nature of dharma is difficult to grasp, and if one prioritises the wrong aspect of it, social disorder is allowed to flourish. The same act that was righteous in one situation can destroy the stability of the social cycle in another. This is truly relevant today. A bird’s-eye view of certain decisions made by those in positions of power may sound righteous on paper, but in practice it leads to outcomes that are not acceptable.
